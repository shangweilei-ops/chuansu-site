The problem I started with

Most household fires happen in the most "ordinary" scenes — kitchen oil fires, electrical ignition, power-bank self-combustion. The extinguisher exists, but it's rarely at hand. The fire hydrant exists, but ordinary people don't know how to use it. The bottleneck is not technology, it's product.

What SafeHome is

SafeHome is a family of fire-suppression product prototypes for households and dorms, including:

  • Fire-retardant coating: applied to common combustible surfaces
  • Suppression patch: peel-and-stick emergency device for high-risk points (lithium batteries, outlets)
  • Emergency kit: a "30-second-to-activate" response pack designed for dorms and households

Where it's been deployed

  • Shenzhen Middle School campus dorm pilots
  • Multiple Shenzhen community fire-safety stations
  • Several real household field tests
  • Maling community service center — "Before the Spark" themed outreach
  • School "Parents into the Classroom" lecture series

Design principle — lower the friction, not just the temperature

"If a person has to think for more than three seconds, they won't use it. Products win by reducing decision cost, not by being correct."

The deepest lesson from SafeHome isn't in materials science — it's in behavioral design. Whether a product gets used is almost independent of its technical specs; it depends on its psychological friction: where it's stored, how long it takes to retrieve, whether it dirties anything, whether it looks safe enough.

That's why I later started Chemisfun seriously — if people's cognition doesn't catch up, even a perfect product won't be reached for.

What this taught me about being an applicant

Building SafeHome taught me one thing: applying to college isn't stacking activities that "look right" — it's pushing one thesis from material layer all the way to the human layer. Every decision I made inside SafeHome answers the same question as every sentence I write in Chemisfun, and every hydrogel molecule I touch in the Garcia Lab.
