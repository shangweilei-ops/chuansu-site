The biggest gap in fire safety isn't knowing — it's believing

2026 · 07 · Essay

A fire drill in 9th grade was the seed of every project I've built since.

I research fire safety, I run science communication, I know the danger — but the first second after the alarm, my reaction wasn't "evacuate now." It was "this can't be real." That second taught me more directly than any paper: the real gap is not knowing, it's believing.

After that I built SafeHome, putting fire-suppressant materials into dorms and communities. Then Chemisfun, translating complex chemistry for people willing to believe. Then the Garcia Lab, pushing the same material thinking from environment into the body.

Three years on, I'm increasingly sure: the victory of a material or a product ultimately happens inside someone's head. That's why — when I sat down to write the main essay — I started from that one second.

"The biggest gap in fire safety isn't knowing — it's believing. My work is to close that gap — one material, one product, one honest sentence at a time."

Hydrogel isn't a new material — it's a new material view

2026 · 06 · Research Note

At the molecular level, fire-retardant coatings and hydrogel tissue repair are very different — one is a flame-inhibiting polymer, the other a hydrophilic crosslinked network.

But in my first year in the Garcia group, I started to realize they were doing the same thing: protecting fragile systems under extreme conditions.

A molecular design that survives thermal runaway under flame often also survives chemical attack in a wound's inflammatory environment. A structural idea that blocks heat conduction can often be translated into a physical barrier against cytokine diffusion.

I started treating "hydrogel" as a material view, not a new material — a set of strategies for extreme conditions, applicable to different objects.

Why SafeHome isn't just a "fire-suppression product"

2026 · 04 · Product Note

Whether a fire-suppression product gets used is almost independent of its technical specs — it depends on its psychological friction.

Where is it stored? How long does it take to retrieve? Will it dirty anything? Does it look safe enough? A solution that asks someone to think for more than three seconds won't be activated by most people in a real fire.

That's the core lesson I've taken into product design: products win by reducing decision cost, not by being correct.

Which is also why I later got serious about Chemisfun — if people's cognition doesn't catch up, even the best product won't be reached for.

Chemisfun's writing rules: how I explain one molecule to everyone

2026 · 02 · Craft

Don't cut the jargon, don't dumb it down, don't exaggerate. Be the honest friend.

  • Don't cut the jargon — explain the term clearly, don't dodge around it.
  • Don't dumb it down — readers are smarter than I think.
  • Don't exaggerate — danger has been exaggerated too many times; the cost of believing has been burned too many times.
  • Structure first — every piece must answer one specific question.

I treat Chemisfun as intellectual vitality training — not "I learned knowledge", but reorganizing, transmitting, and changing how others understand it.

Why I added an AI paper to a materials science site

2026 · 08 · Personal Note

People sometimes ask why an "AI for drug discovery" paper sits on a "materials for fragile systems" site. The answer is the same as always.

A brain in early Alzheimer's is a fragile system too. Drug repositioning is the same question at another scale: can a small, targeted intervention protect the system before the damage becomes visible? Multi-modal deep learning with uncertainty quantification is just the tool I had access to.

More importantly — the paper was my first cross-disciplinary attempt, and it taught me that the same thesis can survive across fields, as long as the question stays honest.

A note on my 27Fall application strategy

2026 · 09 · Application Note

I get asked why I'm applying to 7 schools across the US + Hong Kong. The short answer: I'm not optimizing for a single outcome. I'm choosing environments, not prizes.

Stanford REA is the reach — the place where a single thesis across material, product, and human is exactly the kind of intellectual identity they're looking for. Johns Hopkins ED2 is the research-fit — the kind of place where a freshman can walk into a lab and start pushing a question. UC Berkeley is the entrepreneurial version of the same thesis.

Northwestern is a deliberate addition in September — the engineering + materials + design intersection drew me in. UPenn fits the product + impact side. The UC system gives me room to keep all options open without losing the REA/ED2 bet.

And HKUST IRE is the Asia parallel — research-enrichment path, with a real shot at scholarship. It treats undergrads as near-researchers, which is what I want to be. If the US path lands me in a top-30 without funding but HKUST comes back with IRE + full scholarship, the choice is more rational than emotional.

The full list, in round order: Stanford REA → JHU ED2 → HKUST IRE → UCB / Northwestern / UPenn / UC system (RD).
